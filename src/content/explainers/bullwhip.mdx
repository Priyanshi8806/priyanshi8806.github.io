Picture a chain of four companies: a **retailer** sells to customers, orders from a **wholesaler**, who orders from a **distributor**, who orders from a **factory**. Each one sees only the orders of the company directly below it.

Customer demand is fairly steady, around 100 units a week with some noise. Yet the factory sees orders that swing far more wildly. This is the **bullwhip effect**, and it costs firms money through excess inventory, idle capacity and stock-outs.

## Try it

Each tier forecasts demand with a moving average of the orders it receives, then orders enough to cover the expected demand over its lead time. Drag the sliders and watch the darker (upstream) lines.

<Bullwhip />

## What's going on

Three things to notice:

1. **Longer lead times make it worse.** When restocking takes longer, each tier has to react more strongly to any change in its forecast.
2. **Short forecast windows make it worse.** A short moving average chases noise, so every random blip looks like a trend.
3. **Sharing customer demand helps, but doesn't eliminate it.** When every tier forecasts from the *customer's* demand instead of the orders it receives, amplification grows roughly linearly along the chain instead of multiplying at each step.

The tick marks in the second chart show the other scenario, so you can compare *with* and *without* sharing directly.
